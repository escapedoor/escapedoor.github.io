What you now know
  1. Long English words are built from parts: a root carries the meaning, a prefix goes in front and a suffix at the end (in-vis-ible). A word you have never seen can be worked out from its parts.
  2. Many roots are Latin (aud, dict, leg, port, script, spect, vis) or Greek (tele, graph, phon, chron, micro, auto). Not every part is what it looks like: there is no ear in early, no ham in hamburger, and no knight in sirloin.
  3. English let words in by many doors: Old English (cow, shirt), Old Norse (sky, skirt, window), French after 1066 (beef, sirloin), Latin and Greek for learning (television), and trade and travel (shampoo, ketchup, kindergarten).
  4. On the farm the animals kept their English names; on the Norman table the meat got French ones: cow and beef, pig and pork, sheep and mutton. One old word can come in by two doors and become two words: shirt and skirt.
  5. A false friend looks like a word you know and means something else: French actuel and sympathique, and Korean English hand phone, notebook and Fighting!
  6. A false etymology is a good story about a word that is not true. Test it: is the word older than the story? Does it explain an old word by initials? Does it have a famous person and a neat ending? Trust the dated slip, not the teller.
  7. A dictionary entry has a headword, a pronunciation, a part of speech, senses, an etymology in square brackets and a date of first known use, and every part of it rests on evidence.
