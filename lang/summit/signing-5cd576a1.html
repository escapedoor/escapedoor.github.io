What you now know
  1. Simultaneous interpreters work in soundproof booths, each booth into its own language, a few seconds behind the speaker. They work in pairs and change about every half hour, because after that their mistakes grow. In consecutive interpreting, the interpreter speaks after the speaker, from notes.
  2. When nobody in a booth knows the speaker’s language, the booth takes relay from another booth, and any mistake travels with it.
  3. An idiom means more than its words. Put word for word into another language, it becomes nonsense or an insult. Translate the meaning, with another idiom or in plain words, and watch out for idioms that look the same and mean something else.
  4. Register is how formal your words are. The same request can be casual, neutral or formal, and a treaty is the stiffest of all: shall for a duty, may for a choice, numbers instead of “soon”, no idioms.
  5. What is said is not always what is meant. Understatement and polite indirectness can say no without saying it, and in diplomatic English “we note” is not “we agree”.
  6. A sentence is ambiguous when it can be read in two ways, because of one word or because of its structure. Mend it by moving or changing words until only one reading is left.
  7. False friends can hide in treaties: French éventuellement means possibly, English eventually means in the end. In 1889 one article of the Treaty of Wuchale gave a choice in Amharic and a duty in Italian, and it ended in war.
