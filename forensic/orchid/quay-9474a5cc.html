CLEARED · CASE CLOSED

What you now know
  1. CITES (agreed in 1973, in force since 1975) controls the trade in endangered wild plants and animals. Wild-collected plants of an Appendix I species, like slipper orchids, may not be traded for profit; nursery-grown ones may cross a border with a certificate.
  2. A plant dug up in the wild gives itself away by several signs together: torn roots, grit and old leaves, scars, moss, no label. One sign alone proves nothing.
  3. A tread pattern only tells you the make of a tyre. Wear, cuts and stones in the grooves make one tyre different from every other, so a print must match those too.
  4. Mud on a vehicle builds up in layers, the newest on the outside. The chain of custody records every sample and every hand-over, with a time, and a time can put a mistake right.
  5. Every plant makes pollen of its own shape. Pollen and the soil’s colour, texture and grains make a fingerprint of a place; the season changes the mix; and a sample shows where something has been, and where it has not.
  6. In DNA, A pairs with T and C with G, so one strand gives you the other. A short barcode region, compared base for base with reference sequences, can name a species.
  7. The evidence was honest all along. A matching pattern, a leaf on a floor and a number on a lid all misled, until somebody checked what had really happened to them.
