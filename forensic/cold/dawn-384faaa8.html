SENT TO THE COMMISSION · 07.00

What you now know
  1. Chain of custody: every hand that holds an exhibit signs for it, with the date and the time, and every seal on the bag must be on its label. A gap does not prove that anything went wrong, but it lets doubt in; other papers (a day book, seal numbers, sizes) can show what really happened.
  2. When glass is struck, radial cracks run out from the point of impact and concentric (ring) cracks run round it. On a radial crack the rib marks meet the side away from the blow at a right angle: the 3R rule. On a ring crack it is the other way round.
  3. A crack stops where it meets an earlier crack, so the cracks give the order of two blows, and even the order of the cracks of one blow.
  4. Under the microscope, wool has scales, cotton is a flat twisted ribbon and nylon is a smooth rod. Fibres pass from one thing to another whenever they touch, so things from a scene and from a suspect are kept apart; a fibre that matches is only “consistent with” a source.
  5. A DNA profile is a pair of numbers at each of several loci, one number from each parent. If at two loci a person has numbers that the parents could not have given, they cannot be that couple’s child.
  6. “One in 3,000” is how often a stranger would match by chance, not the chance of being innocent: in a town of 60,000 it means about 20 people. Relatives match far more often.
  7. An expert can be honest and still be wrong. A fair review separates what was seen from what was concluded, asks whether the item and the information were the right ones, and says what the methods of the time could not do.
