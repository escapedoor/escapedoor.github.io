Come in by day

What you now know
  1. A draft is a step, not a failure. Between drafts a writer cuts what the reader does not need, adds what the reader must not miss, moves the strongest thing to the first line or the last, and sharpens two vague words into one exact one. STET means “let it stand”.
  2. Drafts can be put in order by their evidence: a note in one margin is an order to the next draft, and the next draft obeys it. Neat is not the same as new.
  3. Irony is a gap between what is said or expected and what is. Verbal irony says the opposite of what it means; situational irony is the opposite of what was expected; dramatic irony is the reader knowing what a character does not. Bad luck and coincidence are not irony.
  4. A twist plays fair when its clues are planted on the page before the turn, in plain sight, and no clue says no. A twist that nothing on the page points to is a cheat. Twists are built out of irony: ask who expected what.
  5. Plot is what happens; a topic is a word or two; a theme is what a story says about people, in a whole sentence that is true outside the story. What survives every draft is the theme, and the ending has to agree with it.
  6. A first-person narrator is not the author, even when the author lends the narrator her own life. The author changes things (an age, a colour, a husband), and she knows the ending before the narrator does: she plants the clues, and the narrator walks past them.
  7. A carbon copy is the second copy a typist makes at the same time as the first, and keeps. That is what “cc” still means at the top of an email.
Twists that play fair, to read next
  • O. Henry, “The Gift of the Magi” (1905): two presents, two treasures given up, and the clues on the first page.
  • Saki, “The Open Window” (1914): a girl of fifteen, a nervous visitor, and a last line Gran says is the best in English.
  • Guy de Maupassant, “The Necklace” (1884; read it in English): ten years of hard work for a borrowed necklace.
  • W. W. Jacobs, “The Monkey’s Paw” (1902): three wishes, each granted in the worst way. Not for reading alone at midnight, even with a red torch.
