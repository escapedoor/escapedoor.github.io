What you now know
  1. Every story has a narrator. A first-person narrator says I and can tell only what she saw; a third-person limited narrator stays with one character, like a lamp she carries; an all-knowing narrator can go into any room and any head. Only an author can be all-knowing.
  2. To test a sentence, ask who could know it. A witness can only ever be first person or limited.
  3. An unreliable narrator may be wrong (she misunderstands what she saw), biased (she sees what she wants to see) or may leave things out on purpose (all she says is true, but not all that is true is said).
  4. Set several accounts side by side. Where they agree, it is probably true; where two cannot both be true, at most one is right. Trust a narrator on what she saw, not on what she thinks it meant.
  5. Foreshadowing is a small detail shown early, in plain sight, that matters later. A red herring looks like a seed and grows nothing. A fair story lets the reader say: I was told, and I did not see it.
  6. An idiom means something different from its words: a storm in a teacup is a fuss about very little, to let the cat out of the bag is to tell a secret by mistake, and to turn over a new leaf is to start again and do better. Taken word for word, idioms make jokes, and sometimes trouble.
  7. Halley’s Comet came back in 1910, the spring that King Edward VII died, and on the night of 18 to 19 May the Earth passed through its tail. Nothing at all happened.
