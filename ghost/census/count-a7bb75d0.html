COUNTED
Not a Soul Missed

On Saturday evening, at five o’clock, Miss Tunstall counted the Lantern Walk at the lych-gate. Fourteen tickets. Fourteen heads. She counted twice, to be sure, and then a third time, because she could.

What you now know
  • Since 1841 (but not in 1941) every household in England and Wales has been counted on one night every ten years: in 1881, Sunday 3 April. The returns stay closed for a hundred years; 1921 opened in 2022, and 1931 was lost in a fire.
  • The enumerator copied each household’s schedule into his book: name, relation to the head, condition, age last birthday in a column for males or for females, occupation, where born. “Do.” means ditto; “3 mos” is an age in months; an age is what the person said.
  • The census counted the living at midnight, where they slept: a child born after midnight, or somebody who slept elsewhere, was not written there.
  • A baptism gives the day of the baptism, not the birth; a burial the day of the funeral, not the death. A marriage says “of full age”, “spinster”, “of this parish”, and afterwards a woman is found under her husband’s name.
  • A stone is cut later, from memory, and can be wrong; a register written that week is better. “Relict of” is widow of; “the late”, “infant son of”, “the above”, “née”.
  • A log book records facts; a diary chooses what to say, and may say it kindly or proudly. Check what can be checked, mark what cannot, and look for what is left out. Compare a doubtful letter or figure with the same writer’s known ones.
  • To prove that somebody was missed, show that she was alive, that she was there, that she was not counted anywhere else, and make the numbers come out right.
