TO PRESS 17.52 · ON THE MAT THURSDAY

What you now know
  1. To test a document, lay it over a genuine one from the same office. The letterhead should match; a reference number and a signature never should, because an office gives each letter its own number and nobody signs twice exactly alike.
  2. Check every date. A weekday moves on one day each year (two after a 29 February), and a document cannot know what had not happened on its date: a title, a logo, a meeting, a number.
  3. A document is made of something: paper (A4 or American Letter), a typeface (serifs or none, one storey or two), pasted pictures that break into pixels, and metadata, which tells you whose computer, not whose fingers.
  4. Line sources up one question at a time: first-hand or second-hand, could they know, independent or not (two copies of one email are one source), and on the record, not for attribution or off the record.
  5. Who gains, or a lie about something else, is a reason to look harder, never proof. To say who made something you need tests a person can fail: access, time and habit.
  6. Hedges are promises: none for what we can prove, “said” for words on the record, “according to” for a record, “is understood” for a source we will not name. “Alleged” and quotation marks do not make an accusation safe: repeating it is publishing it.
  7. In England and Wales the main defences to libel are truth, honest opinion and public interest. Ask the person first (the right of reply), and make the headline and the first paragraph say only what you can prove, because most readers stop there.
