EVERYBODY OUT

What you now know
  1. Before you go underground, fill in the call-out board: who, which cave and entrance, the route, when in and when out, the call-out time (out time plus a margin) and where you will wait if the way floods. Carry three lights each, every one with its own battery.
  2. Rain takes up carbon dioxide and becomes a weak acid that dissolves limestone. Water sinks at sinkholes, goes down the joints and along the bedding planes, runs along the top of the shale and comes out at a resurgence. Where the roof dips under the water, there is a sump.
  3. Stalactites and stalagmites grow from dripping water, very slowly: often less than a centimetre in a hundred years. Mud, foam and twigs show how high floods have come. Being old is not the same as being dry.
  4. A flood comes as a pulse: it rises fast and falls slowly, and it can go on rising for hours after the rain stops. Hours to wait = (reading now − reading when the way opens) ÷ fall in an hour, and that is the earliest time.
  5. A survey shows a cave from above (the plan, with north and a scale) and from the side (the section, with heights). The same numbered stations are on both. A passage that goes round the water in plan can go under it in section.
  6. A cave stays at about the same temperature all year. On a cold night its warm air rises out of the highest entrance; in summer its cold air flows out of the lowest. A draught means another way out, and a candle flame leans the way the air is going.
  7. If the way out floods: never try to pass water that reaches the roof; go up, keep warm, keep one light on, and wait. If you move, leave a note. When you get out, tell the rescue who, where, how you are, and that nobody is left inside.
