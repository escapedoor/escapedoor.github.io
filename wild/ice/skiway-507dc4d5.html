ON THE PLANE

What you now know
  1. An ice sheet flows. Where the ice in front moves faster, the ice is stretched and cracks: crevasses open at right angles to the stretching, across the flow just past the top of a bump and on the outside of a bend, and slanting at about 45 degrees next to rock. Snow bridges hide them, so you rope up, probe, and cross at right angles, never along.
  2. Near a magnetic pole a compass is no use. The field is nearly upright, so the needle dips and sticks; very little of it runs along the ground, so the needle is slow and wanders; and the gap between compass north and true north can be huge: 168 degrees at Camp Yoke.
  3. In a polar summer the sun goes round the sky without setting, about 15 degrees an hour: due north at local noon and low in the south at local midnight. With a sun table and a watch, the sun or your own shadow is a compass. Beware sun dogs, 22 degrees to each side.
  4. The katabatic wind is cold, heavy air sliding off the ice sheet: strongest at night and in the early morning, easing when the sun is high. Its direction is set by the slope, down the fall line, turned to the left in the southern half of the world. It does not blow from wherever you would like it to.
  5. When the drift hides the next flag, stop before you are cold: tent with its back to the wind and its door downwind, warm drinks, keep your skeds, and wait for the late morning.
  6. A confident sentence is only as good as its because. Must, definitely, can’t and won’t need proof; should gives advice; might admits a doubt. A reason that is true in one place may be false in another.
  7. Dead reckoning: your last known point, your heading and your distance tell you where you are when nothing else can.
