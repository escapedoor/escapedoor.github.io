What you now know
  1. A machine that learns is given examples, each with a label that a person wrote: its training data. It believes every label, so a careless label teaches it a wrong lesson.
  2. It finds patterns in the features it can measure (here colour, shine and outline). It does not know what a thing is. It leans on whatever told its examples apart: when every can it saw was shiny, “shiny” became its idea of a can.
  3. A score on the examples it learnt from tells you very little (47 out of 50, 94%). Test it on examples you kept back: new to it, labelled, and like the real job today.
  4. One number can hide a problem. Look at each kind of thing on its own: 50% overall, but 25% for cans.
  5. Lopsided data: if 90 of every 100 examples are paper, a machine that always says “paper” is 90% right and useless. Count each class, balance them, and give every kind of thing it will meet.
  6. The fix was a better set of examples and a person checking, not a cleverer, dearer machine.
  7. Trust a machine like this when it has been fairly tested on things like today’s. Check it when the world changes (a new kind of can) and whenever a mistake would cost a lot.
