What you now know
  1. A radio telescope is a great dish that gathers radio waves at its focus. A bigger dish hears fainter things and sees more sharply. Many people who search for signals listen near the hydrogen line: 1420 MHz, 21 cm.
  2. Test a signal before you believe it: it should go when the dish looks away, stay among the stars, and be a needle, not a hill; then a second telescope should hear it. Fences, satellites and microwave ovens fool people.
  3. A message can teach its own language: counting first, then symbols set between numbers (plus, equals, not, a question), each worked out from the sentences round it.
  4. A number made by multiplying two primes can be laid out as only one rectangle, or the same one turned round: 1679 = 23 × 73 at Arecibo in 1974, and 493 = 17 × 29 tonight.
  5. You can work out a word you do not know from its sentence, and better from several sentences: a second example tells you more than the first.
  6. Some words have two meanings (present, leave, arms), and one language may use one word where another needs two (a tool and a weapon). Only the context decides, and the wrong choice can turn a welcome into a threat.
  7. A light year is the distance light travels in a year. A reply to a star 26 light years away takes 26 years to arrive, and an answer to it takes 26 more.

Say it aloud: explain to a friend, in five or six sentences, how a message can teach its own language, from counting to a picture.
