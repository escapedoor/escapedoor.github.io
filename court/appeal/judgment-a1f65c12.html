CONVICTION QUASHED

What you now know
  1. The Court of Appeal does not try a case again. It asks one question: is the conviction safe? If it is unsafe, the court shall allow the appeal and quash the conviction.
  2. At a trial the prosecution must prove its case beyond reasonable doubt, and the defendant proves nothing. So “not guilty”, or a quashed conviction, means guilt was not proved. It does not mean innocence was proved.
  3. In legal English, shall and must leave no choice; may means the court can choose. The court may receive fresh evidence if it can be believed, could help the appeal, could have been used at the trial, and there is a good reason it was not.
  4. Memory is built again every time it is told. A leading question, a photograph seen later, or “Well done, that’s our man” can change it. The first account, given before anybody suggested anything, is usually the one to test, and confidence is not proof.
  5. Six bad arguments: attacking the person, the straw man, the false choice, after it so because of it, the appeal to authority, and arguing in a circle. Name it, then say exactly why it fails.
  6. Check every clock against something whose real time is known: a camera that was never put forward for summer time is an hour behind.
  7. Find the hole in your own case before the other side does, and tell the truth about it. A barrister must never mislead the court.
