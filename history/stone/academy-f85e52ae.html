READ TO THE
ACADÉMIE
Paris · 14 to 27 September 1822

What you now know
  1. The Rosetta Stone carries one decree of 196 BC three times: in hieroglyphs, in Demotic and in Greek. The Greek could be read, and its last line says the three are one text.
  2. To match words across scripts, count and keep the order: a word that comes three times in the Greek comes three times in the others. The commonest group is not always the name you expect.
  3. A ring (a cartouche) holds a royal name. Laid side by side, the rings of Ptolemy and Cleopatra share the signs for P, O and L, each where the Greek spellings need it: so the signs stand for sounds.
  4. One sound may have two signs (the half-loaf and the hand for T, as French writes K with C, K or QU), and short vowels are often left out.
  5. Some signs have no sound. The egg after a queen’s name says “a woman”, and the half-loaf before it is the T that Egyptian puts on words for women.
  6. Once the sound signs are known, a new name can be read (Alexander), and Coptic, the last form of Egyptian, unlocks words: RĒ, the sun, and MISE, to give birth, in the name of Ramesses. Hieroglyphs mix sound signs and word signs: each picture is not a word, and each sign is not a letter.
  7. A source is worth what its writer could know on the day it was written. Read its date in its own calendar first.
Jean-François Champollion read his “Letter to M. Dacier” on the sound signs to the Académie des Inscriptions on 27 September 1822; by tradition, on 14 September he ran to his brother’s office crying “Je tiens l’affaire!” and fainted. Thomas Young had read the name of Ptolemy first; William Bankes’s obelisk gave Cleopatra. Champollion set out the whole system, sound signs and meaning signs together, in a book of 1824. Vigneau, Zélie and Madame Solange Thorel, Madame Séraphine Usson and M. Xavier Sautel are invented, and so is their part: in 1822 Champollion did this work himself. ·
